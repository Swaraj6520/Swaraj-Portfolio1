LinkedInTechnicalText post + 1 photo

Why is this still done by hand?

Written for the engineer who specs harnesses. I pulled the hard problems from your own engineering job posts.

Your robot has a part inside it that was built by hand.

Not the motors. Not the boards. The wiring.

The wire harness is one of the last big manufacturing processes still done almost entirely by hand. Four reasons it resisted automation:

  1. Wire is limp. Robots love rigid parts. Wire droops, twists, and never sits in the same place twice.
  2. Connectors are tiny and shiny. Finding one terminal in clutter, under real factory lighting, is a hard vision problem.
  3. Seating is sub-millimeter. Close does not count.
  4. Every job is different. High mix. One unit on this order, ten thousand on the next.

So the industry kept doing it by hand, and the lead time settled at 5 to 7 weeks.

We build our own machines and our own quoting software to change that. Custom harness, about 7 days.

Series that repeat
01

Lucas walks the line.

He already explains the process well in the launch video. Same energy, on the floor: how a harness gets made, start to finish, in one take per station.

02

Meet the machine.

A spotlight, but for a robot. One machine per episode: what it does, what it replaced, what it still gets wrong. The engineer who built it does the talking.

Factory build log.

20,000 square feet going from empty to running. A short update every time something big arrives, gets bolted down, or turns on for the first time.

04

People of the floor.

Technicians and engineers in their own words. One question each, answered at their station, never in a conference room.

How it keeps flowing
01

One floor day feeds the week.

Wednesday is for filming. Everything else is editing, writing, and publishing. The calendar never depends on catching something by luck.

02

Fifteen minutes with engineering, every week.

Three questions: what broke, what shipped, what surprised you. That is where the technical posts come from, and it costs engineers almost nothing.

03

One slot stays empty.

Trends do not book ahead. Friday's short-form slot is open by design, with an evergreen clip ready if nothing is worth jumping on.

What I'd track

Reach is the funny video's job. Quotes are the factory's.

Short-form gets judged on whether people stay past the first three seconds and whether they share it. LinkedIn gets judged on who shows up, meaning comments and follows from engineers at hardware companies, not raw impressions. YouTube on how long people watch. And one number sits across all five: clicks through to the instant quote, tagged per platform, so you can see which content brings in people with a drawing in hand. Two weeks on a new account is too little data to kill anything, so early Fridays are for reading the numbers and reworking the weak formats. Cuts come once there is enough to trust.

The thinking
01

The funny one and the serious one share a fact.

"Faster than a quote" and the LinkedIn post both run on the same lead-time table. Different audience, different register, same truth.

02

The joke points at the wait.

Never at technicians, never at customers. The people on your floor are the reason the content is worth watching, so they are the heroes of it.

03

Accuracy before reach.

Every number on this page comes from loombotic.com or your job posts. Anything I could not verify from outside is marked to confirm on the floor.
