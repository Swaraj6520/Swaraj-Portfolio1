If I spent a week inside Nori's San Francisco warehouse, these are the stories I'd look for first: the things engineers are testing, the moments the robot surprises you, the stuff that breaks, and the small details that make a $1,688 robot interesting.

The posts

Same robot. Different reason to stop scrolling.

I wouldn't copy-paste one announcement everywhere. X gets the sharp observation or the weird moment. LinkedIn gets the context. Video gets the thing people need to see with their own eyes.

X · single post
Goal: product curiosity

$1,688 is the headline. The tradeoffs are the story.

Nori A3 is $1,688. 7+1 DOF per arm. 1.5 kg payload per arm. 4 cameras. lidar. 6–8 hour battery. Built in San Francisco. The interesting question isn't “how cheap can you make a robot?” It's “what do you refuse to cut?”
Why I'd post itThe price gets attention already. This turns it into an engineering conversation instead of another product announcement.
LinkedIn · text + workshop photo
Goal: engineering story

Cheap robots aren't useful if nobody can build on them.

Most robotics demos end when the video ends. The more interesting question is what happens when the robot gets into someone else's hands. Nori A3 is built around that idea: a $1,688 robot, an app to train and operate it, and a Skills Marketplace so what one person teaches can eventually travel further than one machine. Affordability gets more robots into the world. More robots means more people trying things we would never think to test ourselves. That's the part I'd be watching closely.
Why LinkedInThis needs a little more context than an X post. It connects the price point to the bigger product thesis without sounding like a brochure.
X / Reels · recurring video
Goal: build-in-public

What broke this week?

No polished launch video. One engineer, one problem, one fix. The exact story changes every week because the work does.

0–03s

Close-up of the robot doing the thing badly. Text: “What broke this week?”

03–08s

Engineer: “We thought it was the arm. It wasn't the arm.”

08–15s

Show the actual failure or test setup. Keep the messy bench in frame.

15–24s

Run it again after the fix. End on whether it worked, not a corporate CTA.

Why I'd make itRobotics is unusually good for build-in-public because failure is visual. The series gives engineers a reason to explain real work without asking them to “make content.”
X · community prompt
Goal: comments + ideas

You get a Nori A3 for 48 hours.

You get a Nori A3 for 48 hours. What is the first completely unnecessary thing you teach it? Best answer gets attempted in the warehouse.
Why I'd post itIt gives the audience a reason to participate, and the comments become a backlog for future demos. I'd only promise the warehouse attempt if the team is actually willing to do it.
Reels / TikTok / Shorts · 15–20 sec
Goal: visual proof

Teach the robot one boring thing.

The less cinematic the task, the better. Loading one dish, fetching one item, folding one towel. The point is watching the learning loop, not pretending the robot lives in a sci-fi movie.

SHOT 1

Human does the task. Text: “Teach Nori this.”

SHOT 2

Quick glimpse of the training/operation workflow.

SHOT 3

First robot attempt. Keep the miss if there is one.

SHOT 4

Best attempt. Text: “What should we try next?”

Why I'd make itNori's site already talks about everyday tasks and teaching skills. A simple before-and-after makes that idea understandable without a paragraph of robotics terminology.
LinkedIn · founder / company voice
Goal: company story

“Made in San Francisco” is content, not footer copy.

A robot has a lot of ways to hide the hard parts. A warehouse doesn't. Parts arrive. Something doesn't fit. A test fails. An engineer changes it. Another robot rolls out. We're assembling Nori in San Francisco because the feedback loop matters more to us right now than pretending manufacturing is separate from engineering. I want to show more of that process here: what changed, what failed, and what the next batch taught us.
Before publishingI would confirm the manufacturing rationale with the founders. The public fact is that Nori is assembled in San Francisco; the reasoning above is the story I'd want to interview them about rather than assume.
The warehouse should be the content engine. Not just the backdrop.

How I'd run it

Get close to the work. Then stay out of engineering's way.

I don't think the social person should constantly ask engineers for fully formed “content ideas.” The job is to make it easy for them to hand over raw material.

One capture block

I would reserve one reliable block each week to walk the warehouse, shoot demos, photograph the bench, and collect enough raw material to avoid chasing people every day.

15 minutes with engineering

Three questions: what changed, what broke, what surprised you? That's probably where the strongest technical posts come from.

Platform-native edits

X gets the sharpest observation. LinkedIn gets the story behind it. Short-form video gets the part that is more convincing when you simply watch the robot do it.

Fast fact-check

Every technical post gets a quick owner and approval path. The goal is accurate without turning every caption into a two-day review cycle.

Watch the right numbers

For X: shares, profile visits, relevant followers and replies. For LinkedIn: comments from the right people, saves and follows. For video: first-three-second hold and completion.

Keep what works

I wouldn't kill a format after one weak post. I'd change the hook, footage or framing first, then decide whether the idea itself is bad.
