The editorial decision

Pick the filter that's hardest to fake.

Source
How to vet your Executive Content agency
Tommy Clark, Social Files, September 7, 2026. The issue gives five filters for choosing a partner: who actually writes for you, whether the founder loves the craft, whether the agency qualifies you, how they adapt to tone, and whether you like each other as people.
Audience
B2B founders and marketing leaders evaluating an executive-content partner. Working hypothesis: they're comparing agencies mostly on price and samples, and some have had an engagement that didn't work. I'd check this against real sales-call notes.
Angle
Personal chemistry between an executive and their writer matters because good content depends on candid conversations, useful feedback, and a working relationship that keeps ideas flowing.
One belief to leave behind

Why this angle
It's the filter Tommy stresses most, and it's the hardest to fake: any agency can show samples, but only the writer can show the conversation works. That makes it useful to the reader and explains why Compound runs prospects through a second call.

What's whose: the five filters and the point about chemistry are Tommy's. The audience hypothesis, the drafts, the interview questions, the journey, and the plan are my proposals. Every draft needs Tommy's input and approval before it could be published.

LinkedIn

Proposed executive LinkedIn draft · Subject to voice review

When you compare content agencies, price, samples, and turnaround time are easy to put side by side. The working relationship is harder to assess.

For founder-led content, it might matter most. The question is simple: do you actually like talking to the person writing for you?

Founder-led content runs on conversation. Every couple of weeks you sit down with your writer and talk through what you're seeing in the market, what you disagree with, and what's happening with customers. In between, you trade notes on Slack. That's where the raw material comes from.

Now picture that conversation feeling like a chore. Interviews get pushed. Feedback gets shorter and vaguer. The writer fills the gaps with safe, generic ideas, and the content starts to sound like nobody.

So before you sign, get on a call with the actual writer, not the founder or the salesperson. Ask how they'd adapt to your voice, and notice whether it feels like a conversation or a pitch.

Preview text

A sales call can't show you what working together will feel like. These questions can help.

Opening

Hey!

In September I wrote about five filters for vetting an executive content agency. The last one was the simplest: make sure you like each other as people.

It may also be the hardest to test before you sign. A sales call is built to go smoothly. The real test comes later, say in a content interview after a draft has missed and you need to explain why.

So this week I want to make that filter practical. Below are six questions to ask the person who will actually write for you. They aren't trick questions, and there's no single right answer. What you're listening for is how they think about working with you: how they pull ideas out of a conversation, how they handle vague feedback, and whether they'll push back when something isn't working.

A strong writer should be able to answer these comfortably, and might ask you a few questions back. If someone sounds rehearsed, or keeps steering back to deliverables and post counts, that's worth noticing too.

Use them on your next call. Borrow them for your own hiring. Forward them to whoever on your team is running the search.

About 200 words

Swaraj's proposed extension · not from the source article
  1. "Walk me through our first content interview."Shows whether they have a real process for pulling ideas out of you.
  2. "What do you do when my only feedback is 'this doesn't sound like me'?"Tests how they turn vague feedback into something usable.
  3. "How much of my time will you honestly need each week?"A realistic answer is a better sign than a low one.
  4. "If you disagree with one of my edits, what happens?"You want a writer who will push back with a reason, not one who folds or digs in.
  5. "How do you like to work between interviews?"Reveals whether day-to-day communication will feel easy or forced.
  6. "What would make you tell us this isn't working?"Good partners can name the warning signs early.

Next step for the reader

Before your next agency call, pick two of these questions and ask them to the writer, not the salesperson. Then compare how each conversation felt, not just what each proposal costs.

A list of questions is something people save, forward to a colleague, and bring into a call. It also follows up directly on a previous issue, which rewards existing subscribers and gives the LinkedIn post a useful place to point.

What video adds

The argument is about how a conversation feels. Text can describe that. Video can show it: tone, pace, and the moment a good question draws out a better answer. That's why the brief centers on a real or staged interview rather than Tommy reading the LinkedIn post to camera.

One experiment, and its limits

Practical checklist vs. point of view. Over several weeks, alternate comparable LinkedIn posts between a checklist framing ("six questions to ask") and a point-of-view framing ("why chemistry beats samples"). Keep topic, length, and posting time as similar as possible, and compare comments from the target audience and clicks to the newsletter.

Limits. A handful of posts is a small sample. Timing, topic, and platform changes can swing results more than framing does. I'd treat the outcome as a direction to test further, not a rule, and read the numbers alongside what people actually say in comments and replies.
