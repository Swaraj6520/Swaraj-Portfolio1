Why is this still done by hand?

For the engineer who specs harnesses. The four reasons come from your Vision/Robotics and Mechanical Design job posts, mostly in your own words.

Your robot has a part inside it that was built by hand.

Not the motors. Not the boards. The wiring.

The wire harness is one of the last big manufacturing processes still done almost entirely by hand. Four reasons it resisted automation:

  1. Wire is limp. Robots love rigid parts. Wire droops, twists, and never sits in the same place twice.
  2. Connectors are tiny and shiny. Finding one terminal in clutter, under real factory lighting, is a hard vision problem.
  3. Seating is sub-millimeter. Close does not count.
  4. Every job is different. One unit on this order, ten thousand on the next.

So the industry kept doing it by hand, and lead times sat at 5 to 7 weeks.

We build our own machines and our own quoting software to change that. Custom harness, about 7 days.

About ten posts a week. I'd rewrite each one for the platform instead of cross-posting.

Series I'd pitch
  • Lucas walks the line. He explains the process well in the launch video. I'd put him on the floor and have him walk a harness from start to finish, one station at a time.
  • Meet the machine. An employee spotlight, but for a robot. What does this one actually do, what did it replace, and what does it still get wrong? The engineer who built it does the talking.
  • Factory build log. You have 20,000 square feet going from empty to running. A short update whenever something big arrives or turns on for the first time.
  • People on the floor. One question per person, filmed at their station.
How I'd run it

One day a week on the floor filming. The rest is editing, writing and posting. I'd also ask for fifteen minutes with engineering each week to find out what broke, what shipped and what surprised them, because that's where the technical posts will come from.

For short-form I'd watch whether people stay past three seconds and whether they share it. On LinkedIn I care more about who comments and follows than about impressions. Across all of it, the number I'd report is clicks to the instant quote, tagged by platform.

Two weeks on a new account isn't enough to judge a format, so early on I'd rework the weak ones before cutting anything.
